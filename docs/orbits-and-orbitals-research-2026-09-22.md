## Recommended story

**Why don't planets orbit in flower shapes?** Start with a real planetary trajectory alongside a lobed electron orbital. Reveal that the pictures encode different things, then explain the wave patterns. A possible follow-up is **The Sun and atoms share a mathematical language**.

The user's intuition has a defensible basis: attraction, symmetry, angular momentum, and waves recur across scales. Similar-looking pictures alone do not establish the same mechanism.

## Claim-to-source research map

| Question | Explanation and production implication | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Are most planetary orbits very oblong? | The eight planets have nearly circular ellipses. A circle is the zero-eccentricity special case. Tilted views make circles appear oval; illustrations often exaggerate eccentricity. Show a camera change before changing the actual orbit. | [NASA: orbits and Kepler's laws](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/orbits-and-keplers-laws/); [NASA: what is an orbit?](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/orbits/en/) |
| Why does a planet stay in a plane? | In the ideal isolated two-body problem, central gravity produces no torque about the center of relative motion. Conserved angular momentum fixes the orbital plane. Bound non-collision trajectories are ellipses; a sphere or cylinder describes neither an individual Kepler orbit nor its plane. | [Feynman, gravitation](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_07.html) |
| Why do different planets share roughly one plane? | They formed in a dissipative, rotating disk. This is a separate question from why an individual orbit is planar. Gas interactions, encounters and subsequent evolution help determine eccentricity and inclination. | [NASA: planetary systems](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stars/planetary-system/) |
| What is an electron orbital? | A spatial wavefunction; its squared magnitude gives position probability density. Use repeated measurements on identically prepared systems to build a cloud, not a continuous trail of one electron. A shell groups states with the same principal quantum number, rather than defining a rigid surface. | [MIT: hydrogen wavefunctions lecture notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/5c828fce769b146e5820a7d6ed0abfc2_MIT5_111F14_Lec6.pdf) |
| Why spheres and petals? | Hydrogen's bound wavefunctions have radial and angular factors. The angular factors are spherical harmonics; different angular structures produce different nodal patterns. A rotationally symmetric interaction permits nonspherical states; symmetry does not require each state to be spherical. | [Feynman, hydrogen atom](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/III_19.html) |
| What do the familiar shapes depict? | Common real orbital representations include spherical s states, two-lobed p states, and four-lobed d states; the familiar d-z-squared state has two lobes and a ring. Boundary surfaces visualize a chosen density threshold or enclosed probability, not a physical skin. | [Sheffield Orbitron: 3d](https://winter.group.shef.ac.uk/orbitron/atomic_orbitals/3d/index.html) |
| Why do planets behave classically? | Macroscopic momenta imply minute de Broglie wavelengths. Environmental interactions suppress observable interference between macroscopically distinct alternatives. Quantum mechanics still applies; classical trajectories are an excellent approximation here. | [University of Tennessee: matter waves](https://labs.phys.utk.edu/mbreinig/phys222core/modules/m10/matter_waves.html); [Zurek: quantum-to-classical transition](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0306072) |
| Is there a real atom-to-star connection? | Solar acoustic modes and atomic angular wavefunctions use spherical harmonics. This is shared mathematics arising from spherical geometry, not identical physical waves or evidence that stars are giant atoms. | [Solar oscillation frequency research](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19910034867); [NASA: helioseismology](https://solarscience.msfc.nasa.gov/Helioseismology.shtml) |

## Deeper points for script review

- Classical gravitational attraction and electrostatic attraction between opposite point charges both follow inverse-square laws. Swapping gravity for electricity alone does not explain orbital petals; wave mechanics is essential.
- Ordinary hydrogen has one proton and no neutron. Nuclear positive charge attracts the electron electrically. Avoid saying electrons orbit individual protons and neutrons.
- A drumhead offers an analogy for modes and nodes. It is a two-dimensional vibrating material; an electron wavefunction is a three-dimensional probability amplitude. Explicitly label the analogy.
- An energy eigenstate can have a time-independent probability density. Orbitals should not automatically rotate, pulse or cycle because the electron has energy.
- Hydrogenic orbitals are exact within the usual nonrelativistic one-electron model. Many-electron atoms require interactions and approximate orbital descriptions. Lobed orbitals are not necessarily the silhouette of an entire atom.
- Distinguish probability density, wavefunction sign/phase, and radial probability. Two phase colors never mean positive and negative electron charge. An s cloud is not a hollow balloon.
- More complicated gravitational trajectories exist. Precessing ellipses can trace rosettes over many revolutions; multi-body trajectories can be nonplanar. Their resemblance to petals still compares a time-history curve with a quantum distribution. [Yale galactic dynamics notes](https://www.astro.yale.edu/vdbosch/GalDyn.pdf) were search-index accessible but the direct PDF fetch failed; re-open before using a specific diagram or quotation.
- Keep galaxy shapes out of the first short. A galaxy is an ensemble of stars, gas and dark matter, not a single orbit. Research its collective dynamics separately before extending the analogy.
